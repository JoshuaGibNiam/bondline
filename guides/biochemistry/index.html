Biochemistry for chemistry students

Amino acids, proteins, enzymes, sugars, fats and DNA, explained as the organic chemistry they really are: condensation, hydrolysis and acid–base reactions.

Biochemistry · Updated 21 November 2026

Biochemistry is organic chemistry inside living things. The molecules are bigger, but the reactions are the ones you already know: condensation (joining with loss of water), hydrolysis (splitting by adding water), acid–base chemistry, oxidation and reduction. This guide covers what A Level, STPM, IB, AP, JEE and NEET chemistry students need.

Amino acids and zwitterions

Every amino acid has an amine group and a carboxylic acid group on the same carbon (the α-carbon), plus a side chain R. All except glycine are chiral, and the ones in proteins are the L form. See all twenty in the amino acids list and flashcards.

The acid and the base react inside the molecule, so in water an amino acid exists as a zwitterion: H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻. That is why amino acids are crystalline solids with high melting points and dissolve well in water but not in hexane.

The isoelectric point (pI) is the pH where the amino acid carries no overall charge. Below the pI it is mainly positive; above it, mainly negative. In electrophoresis, amino acids move towards the electrode of opposite charge, and one at its pI does not move at all.

Peptide bonds

Two amino acids join by a condensation reaction: the COOH of one reacts with the NH₂ of the next, losing water and forming an amide called a peptide bond. Repeating this gives a polypeptide, read from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

  • The peptide bond is planar, because the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the C=O. There is no free rotation about the C–N bond.
  • Hydrolysis breaks peptide bonds again: boiling with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl for about 24 hours, or with enzymes (proteases) at body temperature.
  • Two different amino acids can make two dipeptides (Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly). Three different ones give 6 tripeptides.

Denaturation unfolds the protein (heat, extreme pH, heavy metal ions, organic solvents). The primary structure survives, but the shape, and with it the function, is lost. Heat breaks hydrogen bonds; pH changes break ionic bonds by changing which groups are charged.

Enzymes

Enzymes are protein catalysts. The substrate binds in the active site, a pocket whose shape and side chains suit that substrate. In the induced-fit model, the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, straining its bonds and lowering the activation energy.

  • Specificity: enzymes are often stereospecific, so only one enantiomer of a substrate fits.
  • Temperature: rate rises with temperature up to an optimum (about 37 °C in humans), then falls as the enzyme denatures.
  • pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH (pepsin about 2, trypsin about 8), because the charges in the active site must be right.
  • Michaelis–Menten kinetics: at low [S] the rate is first order in substrate; at high [S] the active sites are saturated, so the rate levels off at Vmax. Km is the [S] that gives half of Vmax; a small Km means the enzyme binds its substrate tightly.

Carbohydrates

Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is an aldohexose: an aldehyde with five OH groups. In water, the OH on carbon 5 adds to the aldehyde to form a six-membered ring (a cyclic hemiacetal). This creates a new stereocentre, the anomeric carbon (C1), so there are two ring forms, α and β.

Two sugars join by a condensation reaction to form a glycosidic bond (an acetal, C–O–C). Hydrolysis with acid or enzymes splits it again.

Reducing sugars have a free anomeric carbon, so the ring can open to the aldehyde. They give a brick-red precipitate with Benedict’s or Fehling’s solution and a silver mirror with Tollens’ reagent. Glucose, maltose and lactose are reducing; sucrose is not, because both anomeric carbons are tied up in the glycosidic bond.

Lipids

A triglyceride (fat or oil) is an ester of glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) with three fatty acids.

  • Saturated fatty acids (no C=C) pack closely, so fats like butter are solid. Unsaturated ones have cis C=C bonds that kink the chain, so oils are liquid.
  • Hydrogenation (H₂, Ni catalyst) saturates the C=C bonds to make margarine. Partial hydrogenation can make trans fats.
  • Saponification: boiling a fat with NaOH hydrolyses the esters to glycerol and sodium salts of the fatty acids, which are soaps.
  • Phospholipids swap one fatty acid for a phosphate group. The polar head and two non-polar tails make them form bilayers: cell membranes.
  • Biodiesel is made by transesterification of a triglyceride with methanol.

Nucleic acids

DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide is a phosphate, a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) and a nitrogenous base. Nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds between the 3′-OH of one sugar and the 5′-phosphate of the next, which are condensation reactions again.

Quick answers

Is a peptide bond the same as an amide bond?

Yes. A peptide bond is an amide bond (–CO–NH–) that links two amino acids. It forms in a condensation reaction and is broken by hydrolysis.

Why do amino acids have high melting points?

They exist as zwitterions, so the solid is held together by strong ionic attractions between NH₃⁺ and COO⁻ groups on neighbouring molecules, not just by hydrogen bonds.

What is the difference between α- and β-glucose?

They differ only at carbon 1 (the anomeric carbon). In α-glucose the OH on C1 is on the opposite side of the ring to the CH₂OH group (down in a Haworth projection); in β-glucose it is on the same side (up).

Why is sucrose a non-reducing sugar?

Its glycosidic bond joins the anomeric carbons of both glucose and fructose, so neither ring can open to give a free aldehyde or ketone that could reduce Cu²⁺ or Ag⁺.
