Functional groups in organic chemistry: list, names and structures

A functional group is the part of a molecule that reacts. Molecules with the same functional group react in the same way, which is why organic chemistry is organised around them.

What they look like

Telling similar groups apart

  • Aldehyde or ketone? Look at the C=O carbon. If it has an H on it (end of a chain), it's an aldehyde. If it has two carbons, it's a ketone.
  • Carboxylic acid or ester? Both have C(=O)O. An acid has O–H; an ester has O–C.
  • Ether or alcohol? An alcohol has O–H. An ether's oxygen sits between two carbons.
  • Amine or amide? An amide's nitrogen is attached to a C=O. An amine's isn't.
  • Primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols depend on how many carbons are attached to the C–OH carbon: one, two or three. This decides how they oxidise.

Spotting them in spectra

IR tells you which groups are present: a broad O–H around 3200–3550 cm⁻¹, a sharp C=O near 1700 cm⁻¹, C≡N near 2250 cm⁻¹. See the IR spectroscopy table.

Quick answers

What is a functional group?

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms in a molecule that gives it its characteristic reactions, such as –OH in alcohols or C=O in ketones.

How many functional groups are there in organic chemistry?

School and first-year courses usually cover about 15: alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, arenes, haloalkanes, alcohols, ethers, amines, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, acyl chlorides, amides and nitriles.

What is the difference between an aldehyde and a ketone?

Both have C=O. In an aldehyde the C=O carbon is at the end of the chain and has a hydrogen on it. In a ketone the C=O carbon is joined to two other carbons.
