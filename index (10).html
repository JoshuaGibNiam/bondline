¹H NMR splitting and the n+1 rule

Each signal in a ¹H NMR spectrum is split by the hydrogens on neighbouring carbons. Counting the peaks tells you how many neighbours there are.

Spectroscopy · Updated 3 November 2026

The n+1 rule

A set of equivalent hydrogens with n hydrogens on adjacent carbons is split into n + 1 peaks.

Neighbouring H (n)PeaksNameRelative heights
01singlet (s)1
12doublet (d)1 : 1
23triplet (t)1 : 2 : 1
34quartet (q)1 : 3 : 3 : 1
45quintet1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1
67septet1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1

The heights come from Pascal's triangle.

Three things that don't split

  • Equivalent hydrogens don't split each other. The six H in ethane give one singlet.
  • OH and NH hydrogens usually appear as broad singlets and don't split their neighbours, because they swap quickly between molecules. Shaking with D₂O makes them disappear.
  • Hydrogens more than three bonds apart (H–C–C–H is three bonds) usually don't split each other.

Patterns worth recognising

You seeIt suggests
Quartet (2H) + triplet (3H)An ethyl group, CH₂CH₃
Septet (1H) + doublet (6H)An isopropyl group, CH(CH₃)₂
Singlet (9H)A tert-butyl group, C(CH₃)₃
Singlet (3H) near 2.1 ppmCH₃ next to C=O, with no neighbours
Singlet (3H) near 3.3–3.9 ppmOCH₃

Worked example: ethanol

  • CH₃ at about 1.2 ppm: triplet, 3H. Its neighbour, the CH₂, has 2 H → 2 + 1 = 3 peaks.
  • CH₂ at about 3.7 ppm: quartet, 2H. Its neighbour, the CH₃, has 3 H → 4 peaks. (The OH doesn't split it.) It's further downfield because it's next to oxygen.
  • OH, about 2–5 ppm: broad singlet, 1H. Its position depends on concentration.

Worked example: propan-2-ol

The two methyl groups are equivalent: one doublet (6H) near 1.2 ppm, split by the single CH. The CH is split by six H, giving a septet (1H) near 4.0 ppm. The OH is a broad singlet.
