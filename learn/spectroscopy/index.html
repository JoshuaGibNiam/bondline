How molecules reveal themselves

Chapter 1

Spectroscopy is listening to molecules

Shine energy at a molecule and it only absorbs the amounts that match something it can do: spin, vibrate or move an electron.

Measure which energies are absorbed, and you learn what the molecule is made of.

Try it

Different light, different job

Pick a kind of radiation and watch what it does to the molecule.

Higher energy (shorter wavelength) can do bigger things: radio only flips nuclear spins, while UV moves electrons.

Chapter 2 · IR

A bond is a spring

Atoms joined by a bond vibrate like balls on a spring. Each bond absorbs infrared at its own frequency.

Atom bonded to carbon:

Stronger bonds and lighter atoms vibrate faster. That is why C–H and O–H are at the high end (above 2800 cm⁻¹) and C=O sits near 1700. (The numbers here come from a simple spring model, so they are close but not exact.)

Read it

Reading an IR spectrum

Peaks point down, because the spectrum shows light that gets through (transmittance). Keep scrolling to sweep across ethanol’s spectrum.

Look for:

Broad O–H at 3200–3550 (alcohol) or very broad 2500–3300 (acid).
Sharp C=O at 1680–1750.
C–H just below 3000.

Below 1500 is the fingerprint region: unique to each molecule but hard to assign by hand.

Chapter 3 · Mass spec

Smash it and weigh the pieces

An electron beam knocks an electron off the molecule, making the molecular ion M⁺. Its m/z (mass ÷ charge, and the charge is +1) gives the relative molecular mass.

The ion often breaks up. Only charged pieces reach the detector. Scroll to fire the beam at 2-chloropropane.

The tallest peak is the base peak: the most stable fragment, here C₃H₇⁺ at 43.

Try it

The M+2 peak gives halogens away

Chlorine and bromine each have two common isotopes, two mass units apart. That gives a second molecular ion peak at M+2.

Learn the ratios: 3 : 1 means Cl, 1 : 1 means Br.

Check

Which halogen?

Peaks at m/z 136 and 138 are the same height. What does the molecule contain?

Chapter 4 · NMR

Hydrogen nuclei are tiny magnets

A ¹H nucleus has spin, so it behaves like a small bar magnet. Scroll to switch on a strong magnetic field, B₀.

The spins line up with the field (lower energy) or against it (higher energy). A pulse of radio waves with exactly the right energy flips them, and as they relax back they give off the signal.

Try it

Chemical shift: who is next door?

Electrons around an H shield it from the field. An electronegative neighbour pulls electrons away, so the H is deshielded and appears further left (higher δ).

Shifts are measured in ppm from TMS (δ = 0). Typical: alkyl 0.9–1.7, next to C=O 2–2.7, next to O 3.3–4.5, alkene 4.5–6.5, aromatic 6.5–8, aldehyde 9–10.

Try it

Splitting: the n + 1 rule

Hydrogens on the neighbouring carbon split a signal. With n equivalent neighbours you get n + 1 peaks, in the ratios of Pascal’s triangle.

H on O and N usually don’t split or get split (they swap too fast), so an O–H is a singlet.

Read it

Integration counts the hydrogens

The area under each signal is proportional to the number of H that make it. Scroll to draw the integration traces for ethanol.

Steps of 3 : 2 : 1 tell you CH₃, CH₂ and OH. Together with shift and splitting, that’s a full assignment.

Check

Solve the spectrum

C₃H₇Cl gives a 6H doublet at δ 1.5 and a 1H septet at δ 4.1. Which isomer is it?
