Chapter 1

Your hands are mirror images

Your left and right hands are mirror images, but you can’t stack one exactly on top of the other. Molecules can be like that too.

On the left is bromochlorofluoromethane, CHBrClF. On the right is its reflection. Drag nothing: they rotate on their own.

Four different groups = a chiral centre

A carbon with four different groups is a stereocentre. Its mirror image is a different molecule, called its enantiomer.

Swap the bromine for a second fluorine, then try again:

With two identical groups the mirror image can be turned to match: the molecule is achiral.

Chapter 2 · R and S

Rank the groups (CIP rules)

To name each enantiomer, rank the four groups by the atomic number of the atom joined to the stereocentre. Higher atomic number = higher priority.

Br (35) > Cl (17) > F (9) > H (1). The gold numbers show the ranking.

If two atoms tie, compare the atoms attached to them, one layer further out, until there is a difference. Double bonds count their atom twice.

Try it

Point the lowest group away, then follow 1 → 2 → 3

Scroll slowly: the molecule turns so that H (priority 4) points away from you. Then trace 1 → 2 → 3.

Clockwise = R (rectus), anticlockwise = S (sinister).

Mirror images always have opposite labels.

Chapter 3

Enantiomers rotate polarised light

Enantiomers have the same melting point, boiling point and NMR spectrum. The one physical difference: they rotate plane-polarised light by the same angle, in opposite directions.

A 50 : 50 (racemic) mixture shows no rotation. Note: R/S does not tell you the sign of rotation; that has to be measured.

Chapter 4

E/Z: geometric isomers

There is no rotation around a C=C, so groups are locked on one side or the other. Rank the two groups on each carbon with the same CIP rules.

Tap to change a group:

Z: the two higher priorities on the same side. E: on opposite sides. Cis/trans only works when each carbon has an H, which is why E/Z is the general system.

Chapter 5

Two stereocentres: enantiomers or diastereomers?

With two stereocentres there can be up to four stereoisomers. Each is drawn as a Fischer projection: horizontal bonds come towards you, vertical ones go back.

Pick two to compare:

Every centre inverted = enantiomers. Only some inverted = diastereomers, which have different physical properties. Tartaric acid has a meso form: two stereocentres, but a mirror plane makes it achiral.
